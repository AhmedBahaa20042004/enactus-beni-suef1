The Raw Material

Shrimp Shells — An Overlooked Resource

Shrimp shells are the hard outer casings left behind after seafood processing. In Egypt — one of the largest shrimp-producing nations in the region — thousands of tonnes of shells are discarded annually by processing plants, restaurants, and households, most ending up in landfills or open dumps.

Yet these shells contain one of the most versatile natural polymers on Earth: chitin — a structural polysaccharide that, when further processed, yields chitosan, a material with extraordinary applications in medicine, agriculture, water treatment, and beyond.

How We Collect the Shells

Enactus New Beni Suef has established collection partnerships with local fish markets, seafood restaurants, and processing facilities across Beni Suef Governorate. Shells are collected fresh to ensure maximum chitin integrity, then transported to our processing site within 24 hours.

Our team provides collection bins and a regular pickup schedule — turning a disposal problem into a community resource loop. Local vendors benefit from free, responsible waste removal, while we gain consistent raw material at near-zero cost.

Environmental Impact

Why Shells Are a Hidden Hazard

When left unmanaged, shrimp shell waste creates serious environmental and public health problems. Understanding these harms is what drives our mission to offer a better solution.

⚗️

Decomposition Odour

Rotting shells emit hydrogen sulfide and ammonia gases, creating severe odour nuisances in fishing communities and near processing sites.

🦠

Pathogen Breeding

Decaying organic waste becomes a breeding ground for bacteria and parasites, posing direct risks to surrounding populations.

💧

Water Contamination

Improperly discarded shells leach nitrogenous compounds into groundwater and surface water, degrading water quality for local communities.

🗑️

Landfill Overload

Crustacean shells are bulky and slow to decompose, consuming valuable landfill space and contributing to solid waste management crises.

🌊

Marine Ecosystem Damage

When dumped in rivers or the Nile, shell waste depletes oxygen levels, harming aquatic life and disrupting delicate ecosystems.

📉

Economic Loss

Billions in potential value from chitin-rich waste are lost every year — resources that could drive rural industry and job creation.

Step 01

Shrimp Shells → Chitin

Chitin is the second most abundant natural polymer on Earth after cellulose. It forms the structural backbone of crustacean shells. Extracting it requires removing two main components bound to the chitin matrix: calcium carbonate (minerals) and proteins.

Chemical Formula: (C₈H₁₃O₅N)n — a long-chain polysaccharide of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine units. Chitin is non-toxic, biodegradable, and biocompatible, making it ideal for downstream applications.

1

Washing & Pre-treatment

Fresh shells are washed thoroughly with water to remove residual flesh, salt, and organic debris. They are then sun-dried or oven-dried at 60°C to reduce moisture content before chemical processing begins.

2

Deproteinisation (NaOH treatment)

The shell material is treated with sodium hydroxide solution (NaOH, ~1M) at 70–80°C for 2 hours, which hydrolyses and dissolves the protein fraction. The residue is washed to neutral pH and filtered.

3

Demineralisation (HCl treatment)

Deproteinised shells are immersed in dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl, ~1M) for 4 hours at room temperature. This dissolves the calcium carbonate mineral fraction, producing calcium chloride and CO₂ as by-products, leaving a pure chitin matrix behind.

4

Decolorisation (Optional)

To produce white or off-white chitin, the material may be bleached with a dilute acetone or potassium permanganate solution to remove astaxanthin and other pigments, followed by thorough washing.

5

Drying & Milling

The purified chitin is dried at 60°C and milled into a fine powder or flake form. Quality control tests measure ash content, protein residue, and degree of acetylation to verify purity.

Step 02

Chitin → Chitosan

Chitosan is produced from chitin through a process called deacetylation — the removal of acetyl groups from the chitin polymer chain. This transforms chitin's limited solubility into chitosan's remarkable water-solubility in mild acidic conditions, unlocking a vastly wider range of applications.

The degree of deacetylation (DD%) is a critical quality parameter — commercial chitosan typically requires a DD of 70–95%. Higher DD generally correlates with better solubility and biological activity.

Key Difference: Chitin has acetyl groups (–NHCOCH₃) on its glucosamine units. Chitosan has free amino groups (–NH₂). This seemingly small change creates dramatically different chemical behaviour and opens the door to remarkable applications.

1

Concentrated Alkali Preparation

Purified chitin is placed in concentrated NaOH solution (40–50% w/v). The high concentration and temperature are essential to drive deacetylation efficiently.

2

Thermochemical Deacetylation

The chitin-NaOH mixture is heated to 100–120°C for 1–4 hours under constant agitation. The alkali cleaves acetyl groups from the N-acetyl-D-glucosamine units, converting them to free amino (–NH₂) groups.

3

Washing & Neutralisation

The product is filtered and washed repeatedly with distilled water until a neutral pH (6.5–7.5) is achieved, removing residual alkali without damaging the newly formed chitosan polymer.

4

Quality Testing — Degree of Deacetylation

DD% is measured via infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), titration, or UV spectrophotometry. Product failing target DD thresholds undergoes a second deacetylation cycle to achieve the required specification.

5

Drying, Milling & Packaging

Approved chitosan is dried at 60°C, milled to the required particle size (fine powder, flake, or granule), quality-labelled with DD%, molecular weight grade, and packed for distribution to end customers.
